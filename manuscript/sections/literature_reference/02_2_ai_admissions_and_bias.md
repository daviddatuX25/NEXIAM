# Chapter 2: Review of Related Literature and Studies

## 2.2 Artificial Intelligence in College Admissions and Algorithmic Bias Mitigation

The integration of artificial intelligence and machine learning into college admissions has emerged as one of the most promising yet heavily scrutinized frontiers in educational data science. As higher education institutions seek objective, scalable methods to evaluate growing applicant cohorts, researchers have documented both the technical capabilities of algorithmic assessment and the critical necessity of mitigating systemic bias.

In a landmark study published in Science Advances, Lira et al. (2023) investigated the application of machine learning models to assess applicant personal qualities in university admissions. Analyzing large-scale applicant cohorts, the authors demonstrated that computational models could identify meaningful behavioral and cognitive patterns that correlate with long-term academic and collegiate success. Crucially, the researchers established that unconstrained predictive models frequently inherit the historical inequities present in training data, producing disparate impacts across socioeconomic and demographic subgroups. However, they demonstrated that use-inspired fine-tuning and rigorous algorithmic audit boundaries could successfully mitigate these performance disparities. Their findings confirmed that while artificial intelligence can provide valuable diagnostic insights, algorithmic tools must be deployed as decision-support aids within governed institutional frameworks rather than as autonomous decision-makers.

The risks of algorithmic bias in higher education selection were analyzed in detail by Van Busum and Fang (2025), who evaluated the behavior of machine learning models applied to undergraduate admissions data. Their research showed that standard classifiers trained on localized historical admission records frequently encode and amplify existing institutional biases, such as favoring applicants from well-resourced urban secondary schools over candidates from rural public institutions who lack advanced elective courses. The authors emphasized that when training datasets are small or demographically skewed, machine learning algorithms tend to latch onto spurious correlations rather than true academic potential. To prevent compounding systemic disadvantage, they recommended that educational models employ strict feature transparency, avoid uncalibrated black-box scoring, and incorporate explicit human-in-the-loop oversight before any recommendation is finalized.

In the Philippine context, applicant decision modeling and enrollment behaviors were explored by Esquivel and Esquivel (2020), who utilized logistic regression to analyze enrollment likelihood at a private Philippine university following the implementation of the K-12 Senior High School curriculum. Their investigation revealed that an applicant's chosen high school academic strand (such as STEM, ABM, or HUMSS) significantly influenced college program choice and subsequent enrollment rates. The authors noted that institutional yield uncertainty often stems from a lack of structured advising: when applicants receive unclear guidance regarding how their high school background aligns with college degree requirements, matriculation rates drop, and students experience higher rates of mid-year course transfers.

These scholarly contributions establish the regulatory and ethical foundation governing NEXIAM. Under Republic Act No. 10173 (Data Privacy Act of 2012), Philippine state institutions carry a legal obligation to ensure that automated data processing does not subject individuals to unlawful discrimination or arbitrary automated decisions. Consequently, NEXIAM explicitly rejects autonomous algorithmic admissions. The platform treats artificial intelligence not as an arbiter of student opportunity, but as a bounded, transparent decision-support mechanism:
1. *Strict Advisory Boundary:* Algorithmic indicators, whether generated through computer-vision OMR scoring or tabular foundation model triage, are categorized as advisory evidence rather than authoritative institutional decisions.
2. *Attributable Human Authority:* Every applicant placement, course recommendation, and enrollment clearance requires explicit confirmation by an authorized human officer (the Guidance Counselor or Registrar Staff).
3. *Auditable Transparency:* System decisions preserve attributable audit trails, recording which data indicators were evaluated, what recommendations were generated, and what human rationale was entered when a counselor accepted or adjusted an advisory indicator.
