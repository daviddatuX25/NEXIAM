# Chapter 2: Review of Related Literature and Studies

## 2.7 The Technology Acceptance Model in Multi-Stakeholder Institutional Systems

To evaluate the operational readiness and stakeholder reception of newly developed information systems, researchers in educational informatics rely on validated behavioral intention models. Among these theoretical frameworks, the Technology Acceptance Model (TAM), formulated by Davis (1989), remains the preeminent and most extensively replicated instrument for assessing user technology adoption.

Grounded in the Theory of Reasoned Action, TAM posits that the acceptance of an information system is determined primarily by two distinct cognitive beliefs:
1. *Perceived Usefulness (PU):* Defined as the degree to which an individual believes that using a specific system will enhance their job performance, administrative productivity, or educational efficiency.
2. *Perceived Ease of Use (PEOU):* Defined as the degree to which an individual believes that operating the system will be free of physical and mental effort.

Davis demonstrated that while system usage is influenced by multiple external variables, Perceived Usefulness and Perceived Ease of Use function as the primary direct drivers of user adoption. In a comprehensive meta-analysis of eighty-eight empirical TAM studies across diverse organizational domains, King and He (2006) confirmed that PU and PEOU exhibit robust predictive validity and statistical reliability across diverse operational contexts. The authors noted that while extended frameworks (such as TAM2 and TAM3) introduce mediating constructs including Attitude Toward Use (ATU) and Behavioral Intention to Use (BIU) for longitudinal adoption studies, Perceived Usefulness and Perceived Ease of Use capture the essential dimensions of user acceptance in single-shot post-implementation evaluations. Consequently, focusing directly on PU and PEOU reduces respondent cognitive fatigue while providing clear diagnostic indicators for software refinement.

In complex institutional environments where a single web platform serves multiple distinct organizational actors, administering an identical, generic questionnaire across all respondents compromises measurement validity. This challenge was addressed by Lippert and Forman (2005), who examined technological adoption across heterogeneous user groups in supply chain and enterprise systems. The researchers established that when different organizational actors interact with fundamentally different functional modules, survey indicators must be adapted to reflect each role's operational reality. Preserving the underlying psychometric constructs of PU and PEOU while tailoring the indicator referents to specific departmental tasks ensures that respondents evaluate features they actually manipulated.

The necessity of role-adapted TAM instruments was further reinforced by Holden and Karsh (2010) in their analysis of multi-stakeholder health and administrative informatics. The authors demonstrated that clinicians, administrative clerks, and end-user clients experience distinct cognitive workflows. Forcing all stakeholders to respond to uniform, abstract statements creates measurement noise and reduces instrument reliability. By adapting item wording to reflect specific tasks (such as record intake for clerks, diagnostic dashboards for specialists, and self-service portals for clients), researchers achieve higher content validity and more actionable evaluation feedback.

These methodological findings directly structure the evaluation design of NEXIAM. Because the platform serves three primary operational stakeholder groups at ISPSC Tagudin Campus (Registrar personnel managing intake and eligibility, Guidance counselors managing scoring and course triage, and first-year students accessing the applicant portal), the evaluation instrument employs role-adapted phrasing. Administered as a single post-test following the execution of role-based simulation scenarios, the instrument captures absolute acceptance levels across Perceived Usefulness and Perceived Ease of Use on a standardized five-point Likert scale, establishing an empirical measure of platform readiness.
